Correlation Functions and Coarsening Dynamics

Spatial correlations and coarsening dynamics are two views of the same spin field. The equilibrium correlation function \(G(r) = \langle \vec{s}_0 \cdot \vec{s}_r \rangle\) encodes the spatial structure of the ordered, quasi-ordered, or disordered phase at a given temperature. When a system is quenched from a disordered initial condition into the low-temperature phase, a characteristic coarsening length \(R(t)\) grows with Monte Carlo time. For a scalar order parameter under non-conserved dynamics the growth law is \(R(t) \sim t^{1/2}\) [1]. For the two-dimensional XY model the vortex-antivortex annihilation that drives coarsening adds a logarithmic correction, \(R(t) \sim (t/\ln t)^{1/2}\) [1] [2]. In the Ising model \(R(t)\) measures the size of domains separated by sharp walls; in the XY model it tracks the spacing between vortices and antivortices rather than any sharp domain boundary.

This notebook treats both topics for the three VibeSpin models: Ising, XY, and the discrete \(q = 6\) clock model. Part I computes equilibrium \(G(r)\) at representative temperatures. Part II follows the coarsening length after a quench. Single-run snapshots and short Ising ensembles are computed inline, while the long multi-seed kinetics traces of all three models are read from cached files written by the ordering_kinetics.py scripts. All stored outputs come from the inline fallback paths (small lattices, one to eight seeds), and the prose says so where the numbers depend on it. The setup cell below imports the required libraries and defines the physical constants and color palette used throughout.

Part I: Equilibrium Correlation Functions

The equilibrium spin-spin correlation function is computed via the structure-factor route: the Fourier transform of the spin field yields \(S(\mathbf{k})\), and radial averaging of the inverse transform gives \(G(r)\) normalized to \(G(0) = 1\). This Wiener-Khinchin approach avoids \(O(N^2)\) real-space pair counting.

Ising Correlations

The Ising model has three qualitatively different correlation regimes. In the ferromagnetic phase (\(T < T_c\)) correlations saturate to a long-range plateau. At the critical point (\(T = T_c\)) the decay is algebraic: \(G(r) \sim r^{-\eta}\) with the exact exponent \(\eta = 1/4\) [3]. In the paramagnetic phase (\(T > T_c\)) the decay is exponential with a correlation length \(\xi(T)\) that diverges as \(T \to T_c^+\).

In the ferromagnetic phase \(G(r)\) is flat at \(G \approx 1\) out to \(r = L/2\). At \(T = 0.5\,T_c\) the squared spontaneous magnetization is about 0.99, and the disconnected correlation function shown here plateaus at that value. At \(T_c\) the curve starts at \(G(1) \approx 0.68\) and follows the \(r^{-1/4}\) guide only for \(r \lesssim 4\); beyond that it bends away above the guide and levels off near \(G \approx 0.4\). This flattening is a finite-size property of the disconnected function, not a deviation from the critical exponent. On a finite lattice at \(T_c\) the configuration carries a nonzero magnetization with \(\langle m^2 \rangle \propto L^{-1/4}\) [3], and \(G(r)\) cannot decay below that level. A direct measurement of \(\eta = 1/4\) needs the connected function or a finite-size-scaling analysis of \(\langle m^2 \rangle\). The paramagnetic curve at \(1.5\,T_c\) decays exponentially, from 0.27 at \(r = 1\) to about \(10^{-3}\) near \(r = 8\). Beyond \(r \approx 10\) it reaches the statistical noise floor near \(10^{-4}\), and the scatter of those points carries no physical information.

XY Correlations

The XY model has algebraic correlations \(G(r) \sim r^{-\eta(T)}\) throughout the quasi-ordered phase below the Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless transition at \(T_{\mathrm{BKT}} \approx 0.893\) [6]. The exponent is set by the renormalized spin stiffness, \(\eta(T) = T / (2\pi \Upsilon_R(T))\). It rises continuously from zero at \(T = 0\) to the universal value \(\eta(T_{\mathrm{BKT}}) = 1/4\), where the stiffness jumps from \(2T_{\mathrm{BKT}}/\pi\) to zero [4] [7]. Only at low temperature, where thermal fluctuations and bound vortex pairs barely reduce the stiffness, does this reduce to the spin-wave form \(\eta \approx T/(2\pi J)\); evaluated at \(T_{\mathrm{BKT}}\) that form would give 0.142 instead of 1/4. At \(T = 0.4\), the lower temperature used below, the spin-wave form gives \(\eta \approx 0.064\), and the leading low-temperature reduction of the stiffness, \(\Upsilon_R \approx J - T/4\), raises the estimate to about 0.071. In either case \(G(r)\) should show only a slight downward slope on log-log axes. Above \(T_{\mathrm{BKT}}\) free vortices proliferate, the stiffness vanishes, and the correlation function decays exponentially, with a correlation length that diverges as \(\exp(b/\sqrt{T/T_{\mathrm{BKT}} - 1})\) on approach to the transition. The low-temperature curve at \(T = 0.4\) is nearly flat on log-log axes, falling only from about 0.88 at \(r = 1\) to 0.74 at \(r = 16\), and the fit gives \(\eta \approx 0.066\). Clock Model Correlations

The \(q\)-state clock model restricts spin orientations to \(q\) evenly spaced angles \(\theta_k = 2\pi k / q\). This notebook uses the discrete implementation DiscreteClockSimulation with \(q = 6\), which stores integer state indices. For \(q \geq 5\) the model has two Kosterlitz-Thouless transitions at \(T_1 < T_2\) and therefore three correlation regimes [5]; for \(q = 6\) the most precise estimates are \(T_1 = 0.7014(11)\) and \(T_2 = 0.9008(6)\) [6]. Below \(T_1\) the six-fold anisotropy is relevant, the system has true long-range order, and \(G(r)\) saturates to a nonzero plateau as in the Ising ferromagnet. Between \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) the anisotropy is irrelevant at long wavelengths and the model flows to the XY fixed line, so \(G(r) \sim r^{-\eta(T)}\) with \(\eta = T/(2\pi\Upsilon_R)\) as in the XY model. The exponent is bounded by \(\eta(T_1) = 4/q^2 = 1/9 \approx 0.111\) at the lower transition and \(\eta(T_2) = 1/4\) at the upper one [5] [6]. At the representative quasi-ordered temperature \(T = 0.8\) the bare spin-wave form \(T/(2\pi J)\) gives 0.127. That is a lower bound, because the renormalized stiffness this close to \(T_2\) lies well below \(J\); the measured value should fall between 1/9 and 1/4. Above \(T_2\) correlations decay exponentially. The cell below loads or computes \(G(r)\) at \(T = 0.5\) (ordered), \(T = 0.8\) (quasi-ordered), and \(T = 1.2\) (disordered); the fallback runs at \(L = 64\) with one seed.

At \(T = 0.5\), below \(T_1 \approx 0.70\), the clock model shows true long-range order: \(G(r)\) is flat at about 0.93 over the whole range, because the spins are locked to one of the six clock orientations and only thermal excitations reduce the correlation below one. At \(T = 0.8\), between the two transitions, the curve is straight on log-log axes from \(r = 2\) to \(r = 16\) and the fit gives \(\eta \approx 0.16\). This lies inside the window \(1/9 \le \eta \le 1/4\) of the \(q = 6\) quasi-ordered phase and above the bare spin-wave value 0.127. Through \(\eta = T/(2\pi\Upsilon_R)\) it corresponds to a renormalized stiffness \(\Upsilon_R \approx 0.77\,J\), a sizeable reduction that is plausible this close to \(T_2 \approx 0.90\). The value comes from one seed at \(L = 64\) and a fit window of less than a decade, so it places \(T = 0.8\) inside the BKT phase rather than measuring \(\eta(0.8)\) precisely. At \(T = 1.2\), above \(T_2\), the correlation function falls from 0.46 at \(r = 1\) to below 0.01 at \(r = 16\) with a concave log-log shape, consistent with exponential decay and a finite correlation length.

The three-regime structure is specific to \(q \geq 5\). The intermediate quasi-ordered phase is the finite-\(q\) remnant of the BKT phase of the XY model: \(T_1\) decreases roughly as \(1/q^2\) while \(T_2\) stays close to \(T_{\mathrm{BKT}}\), so the phase widens with \(q\) and the XY model is recovered as \(q \to \infty\) [5] [6]. For \(q \leq 4\) there is a single second-order transition, but the universality class depends on \(q\). The case \(q = 2\) is the Ising model. The case \(q = 3\) is equivalent to the three-state Potts model with coupling \(3J/2\), with \(T_c \approx 1.49\). The case \(q = 4\) decouples into two independent Ising models with coupling \(J/2\), so \(T_c = 1.1346\) with Ising exponents [6].

Part II: Coarsening Dynamics

When a system is quenched instantaneously from infinite temperature to a temperature inside the ordered phase, the random initial configuration breaks up into locally ordered regions that grow with time. For the Ising model these regions are domains separated by sharp, curvature-driven walls. For the XY model there are no domains in the Ising sense. The random initial state contains free vortices and antivortices, about one per three plaquettes, and coarsening proceeds by their pairwise annihilation, with smooth spin-wave relaxation in between. The \(q = 6\) clock model quenched below \(T_1\) has a discrete order parameter and forms domains of the six clock states, but while those domains are small its texture also contains vortices where several domains meet. In all three cases a characteristic coarsening length \(R(t)\) can be extracted from the spatial correlation structure.

For a scalar or discrete order parameter with non-conserved dynamics, curvature-driven wall motion gives the Allen-Cahn law \(R(t) \sim t^{1/2}\) [1]. This covers the Ising model and, asymptotically, the clock model below \(T_1\), whose walls are expected to dominate at late times as in the Potts model [1]. The two-dimensional XY model is the marginal case. The attraction between a vortex and an antivortex at separation \(R\) falls off as \(1/R\), while the friction on a moving vortex grows as \(\ln R\), so the vortex spacing grows as \(R(t) \sim (t/\ln t)^{1/2}\) and the vortex density decays as \(n_v \sim \ln t / t\) [1] [2]. Bray, Briant, and Jervis showed that this logarithm breaks simple dynamical scaling at and below \(T_{\mathrm{BKT}}\): an ordered initial state relaxes with \(\xi(t) \sim t^{1/2}\), while a random initial state containing free vortices relaxes as \((t/\ln t)^{1/2}\) [2]. A pure power-law fit to XY data over a finite window therefore returns an apparent exponent below 1/2; values near 0.45 are typical of simulations of O(2) systems [1].

Measurement Methodology

The VibeSpin kinetics scripts use random-site Metropolis updates, which preserve physical time evolution, and record three length-scale proxies. \(R_{S(k)} = 2\pi \sum_k S(k) / \sum_k k\,S(k)\) is the inverse first moment of the circularly averaged structure factor. \(R_\xi\) is the interpolated \(1/e\) crossing of the lattice-axis pair correlation \(G(r)\), set to the largest available distance when no crossing exists. The third metric is model-specific: the mean intercept length \(R_{\mathrm{MIL}}\) for Ising, and the vortex density \(n_v\) for XY and clock. Each script runs several seeds (--seeds N, four by default), stores the median trajectory with half the interquartile range as an error bar, and fits a pure power law \(R(t) = A\,t^n\) to the median for \(t \ge t_{\min}\). The median and the interquartile range are insensitive to a single seed that stalls in a metastable configuration. For XY the pure power law is a fit model rather than the predicted law, so its exponent is an effective exponent.

Morphology Comparison: Domains vs Vortices

The two coarsening mechanisms described above produce qualitatively different spatial textures. In the Ising model, the binary spin variable creates sharp domain walls: each site is either \(+1\) or \(-1\), and the boundary between aligned regions is a well-defined one-dimensional curve. In the XY model, the continuous angle variable produces a smooth field that interpolates gradually between regions of different orientation. Domain walls in the Ising sense do not exist; instead, the non-trivial topological objects are point-like vortices (winding number \(\pm 1\)) where the angle field is singular. Vortex Annihilation During XY Coarsening

The smooth angle field shown above hides the topological defects that drive XY coarsening. A vortex is a plaquette around which the spin angle winds by \(+2\pi\) (winding number \(+1\)); around an antivortex it winds by \(-2\pi\). The infinite-temperature initial state is a dense gas of free vortices and antivortices, with one defect per three plaquettes on average for independent random angles. After the quench these free defects annihilate in vortex-antivortex pairs, so the vortex density \(n_v\) falls and the mean spacing \(R_v = n_v^{-1/2}\) grows, asymptotically as \((t/\ln t)^{1/2}\) [1] [2]. The vorticity field, computed from the discrete plaquette winding number, makes the annihilation directly visible. Equilibrium Correlation Length and the End of Coarsening

Parts I and II measure different aspects of spatial order, and this section compares them directly for one Ising quench. The equilibrium correlation length \(\xi_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is the \(1/e\) crossing of the connected correlation function, computed after subtracting the mean spin of each configuration, in an equilibrated simulation. The coarsening length \(R_\xi(t)\) is the \(1/e\) crossing of the disconnected \(G(r)\) of the instantaneous configuration after a quench from \(T = \infty\), and measures the domain size. Three Estimators of the Ising Domain Size

The cell below compares the three Ising length estimators of the cached kinetics run. Each measures the same coarsening process through a different observable. \(R_{S(k)} = 2\pi \sum_k S(k) / \sum_k k\,S(k)\) is set by the mean wavevector of the circularly averaged structure factor. \(R_\xi\) is the \(1/e\) crossing of the pair correlation \(G(r)\) along the lattice axes, and it includes the anti-correlation between neighboring domains on opposite sides of a wall. \(R_{\mathrm{MIL}}\) is the stereological mean intercept length: the lattice size divided by the mean number of wall crossings per row or column. It is a direct chord measure of the domain width and does not depend on a Fourier transform or on the shape of \(G(r)\).

In the scaling regime all three are proportional to the same length, so they should share the growth exponent and differ only in their prefactors [1]. The prefactors, and therefore the vertical ordering of the curves, follow from the estimator definitions and are not universal. In the plot the vertical dotted line marks the fit start \(t_{\min}\), and only points to its right enter the power-law fits. The dotted black reference is a \(t^{1/2}\) line anchored to the \(R_{S(k)}\) trace at the geometric midpoint of the time range.

Vortex Spacing vs Structure Factor and Correlation Length (XY and Clock)

For the XY and clock models the point defects are vortices, so the analogue of the mean intercept length is the mean vortex spacing \(R_v(t) = n_v(t)^{-1/2}\), where \(n_v\) is the number of vortex cores per plaquette. For the XY model the expected laws are \(n_v \sim \ln t / t\) and \(R_v \sim (t/\ln t)^{1/2}\) [1] [2]. A power-law fit to \(n_v\) over a finite window then gives an effective exponent of magnitude below 1, and the derived \(n(R_v) = -n(n_v)/2\) falls below 1/2. For the \(q = 6\) clock model quenched below \(T_1\), vortices are expected to control the texture only while the domains are small; at late times the walls between the six clock states take over.

\(R_v\) counts defects directly and does not rely on a Fourier decomposition or on the shape of \(G(r)\). Plotting it together with \(R_{S(k)}\) and \(R_\xi\) shows whether the spectral, correlation, and defect-counting estimators follow the same growth law. Their relative heights are not fixed by theory. For a continuous order parameter the field varies smoothly between defects instead of jumping across a wall, so the \(1/e\) crossing of \(G(r)\) relates to the vortex spacing through a different, model-specific prefactor than in the Ising case.

Synthesis

The equilibrium correlation functions in Part I characterize the spatial structure that the coarsening dynamics of Part II builds. The Ising model shows the sharpest pattern: long-range order below \(T_c\), algebraic decay at \(T_c\) (visible on \(L = 128\) only at short distances, before the finite-size plateau of the disconnected function), and exponential decay above. The XY model shows algebraic quasi-order below \(T_{\mathrm{BKT}}\) with an exponent set by the renormalized stiffness, \(\eta \approx 0.07\) at \(T = 0.4\) in the fallback run. The \(q = 6\) clock model shows all three regimes, with \(\eta \approx 0.16\) in its intermediate phase at \(T = 0.8\).

In the kinetic regime the growth law depends on the order-parameter symmetry. Domains of a scalar order parameter grow as \(t^{1/2}\) through curvature-driven wall motion. The single Ising quench and the inline ensemble follow this law within their limited range, while the quench-depth comparison shows that prefactors and transients depend on temperature. In the XY model coarsening proceeds by annihilation of free vortex-antivortex pairs, and the log-corrected law \((t/\ln t)^{1/2}\) predicts a vortex density that decays more slowly than \(1/t\); the single-run vortex counts are consistent with this [1] [2]. In the ordered Ising phase the equilibrium correlation length is less than one lattice spacing and does not limit coarsening: the domains grow until they fill the box. The multi-seed exponent comparison across the three models requires the cached kinetics data from scripts/<model>/ordering_kinetics.py.
